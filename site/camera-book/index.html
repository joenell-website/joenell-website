      Chapter 1

      The exposure triangle

      Every photo is a bucket of light. Three settings decide how much light goes in, and each one also changes how the picture looks. Learn what each one does to the image and you can stop guessing.

      Aperture · f-number

      How wide the lens opens

      A small number (f/1.8) is a big opening: lots of light and a soft, blurry background. A big number (f/11) is a pinhole: less light and everything sharp front to back.

      f/1.4 dreamyf/16 all sharp
      Shutter speed · seconds

      How long the light comes in

      Fast speeds (1/1000) freeze a running dog. Slow speeds (2 seconds) turn car lights into ribbons and water into silk. Below about 1/60 you need steady hands or a tripod.

      1/8000 frozen30" streaks
      ISO · sensitivity

      How much the sensor amplifies

      ISO 100 is clean. Each doubling (200, 400, 800…) makes the photo twice as bright but adds grain. Raise ISO only when aperture and shutter can't give you more.

      Higher is brighter, with more grain.

      The pro habit: decide which look matters most first. Want a blurry background? Lock the aperture. Want to freeze or blur motion? Lock the shutter. Then use ISO to make the brightness work. Each full step on any of the three (f/2.8 → f/4, 1/250 → 1/125, ISO 200 → 400) is called a stop, and it halves or doubles the light.

      Six creative tricks that cost nothing

      Shoot through something

      Hold a glass, a prism, leaves or a phone screen right against the lens. The blurry edges add colour and mystery to a plain shot.

      Intentional camera movement

      At 1/8s, sweep the camera up while pressing the shutter on trees. You get a painting, not a photo.

      Zoom burst

      Tripod, 1/2s, twist the zoom ring during the exposure. City lights explode outward from the centre.

      Reflections

      Puddles after rain, sunglasses, car windows, a spoon. Get low and close and flip the world.

      Light painting

      Tripod, 15 seconds, dark street. Draw shapes or words with a phone flashlight while the shutter is open.

      Double exposure

      Many Canon bodies have Multiple Exposure in the menu. On any camera you can stack a silhouette and a texture in an editor.

      Part 2 · Chapter 6

      Joe Studio: amateur to professional

      You don't need a two-thousand-dollar course. You need the camera you already own, a structured plan, a lot of deliberate practice and honest feedback. That's what Joe Studio is built on.

      Shoot daily

      Fifteen focused minutes beats a weekend course you forget by Tuesday.

      One skill at a time

      Each week targets a single skill, with assignments that force you to use it.

      Show your work

      Professionals are made by critique. Post, ask, adjust, repeat.

      The 15-minute daily drill

      1. Pick one setting to practise today: aperture, shutter, focus, white balance or light.
      2. Shoot the same ordinary object 10 ways using only that setting.
      3. Pick your single best frame. Write one sentence on why it works.
      4. Delete the rest without guilt. Curating is a professional skill.

      Habits that make you look professional

      • Shoot RAW, so you can fix exposure and colour later.
      • Check the histogram, not the screen. Screens lie in sunlight.
      • Zoom to 100% on the eyes before you leave a shoot.
      • Back up every card twice: computer plus a drive or cloud.
      • Show 10 great photos, never 100 okay ones.

      Chapter 8

      Gear, editing & first clients

      Buy skills before gear. Upgrade only when a piece of equipment is the thing holding your photos back.

      The budget gear ladder

      Step 1

      Any Canon or Sony body with its kit lens. A used body that is a few generations old produces professional files. Learn it inside out first.

      Step 2

      A 50mm f/1.8 prime (Canon RF 50mm f/1.8 STM or EF 50mm f/1.8 STM, Sony FE 50mm f/1.8). The cheapest way to get blurry backgrounds and clean low-light shots.

      Step 3

      A tripod and a 5-in-1 reflector. Unlocks blue hour, night, astro, waterfalls and flattering portraits.

      Step 4

      One speedlite used off-camera, plus a circular polarizer and an ND filter for landscapes.

      Step 5

      A fast f/2.8 zoom or a second body, only once paid work needs it.

      Editing without a subscription

      Your brand gives you a free RAW editor: Canon Digital Photo Professional and Sony Imaging Edge Desktop. Free open-source options are darktable and RawTherapee. Edit in this order:

      1. Crop and straighten the horizon.
      2. White balance, so whites look white.
      3. Exposure, then highlights down and shadows up.
      4. Contrast and a gentle curve.
      5. Colour: adjust single colours (skin, sky) instead of saturating everything.
      6. Save your look as a preset and reuse it so your portfolio feels consistent.

      Getting your first paid work

      1. Pick a niche you enjoy: portraits, events, food, real estate, pets, products.
      2. Do 3 to 5 free shoots for friends or local businesses in exchange for permission to use the images.
      3. Build a portfolio of your 20 best photos in that niche, nothing else.
      4. Put prices on a simple page: what's included, delivery time, number of edited images.
      5. Use a short written agreement and a model release for every client.
      6. Deliver fast, ask for a review and a referral every time.
